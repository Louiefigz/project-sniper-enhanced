# Clip editing: segments, cleanup and standalone Shorts

Use this to establish the requested edit before selecting or reshaping material
from a conversation, podcast, work session or longer recording. This is the
Producer's editorial playbook; existing source, timing and rendering contracts
still own execution. Reuse the current brief for the creator and format.

## Establish the edit the user wants

Aaron clarified that the nonlinear payoff-first assemblies in this case were
appropriate to this assignment. They are not a default instruction to rebuild
every future clip. A user may want an existing passage, podcast exchange or
set of topic segments, with or without cleanup.

| Edit | What to do |
|---|---|
| Segment / extract | Select or use requested ranges; choose sensible starts and ends while preserving the interior and original sequence. Deliver the requested sections. |
| Clean up / clean up and segment | Tighten filler, pauses, false starts and repetition within the chosen material. Preserve its meaning and conversational order. Split into separate clips only when requested. |
| Build a standalone Short | Search for a strong point and hook, then assemble its context, development and payoff. Join distant passages or reorder them when useful and truthful. |

For a new request whose editorial scope is not already explicit, ask one brief
question before making dependent selection or restructuring decisions. For
example: “Do you want sections kept as-is, cleaned-up excerpts, or Shorts
assembled around one point?” Reuse an explicit instruction or accepted brief;
do not repeat this question on each revision. “Podcast,” “vertical,” “Short” or
“produced” alone does not establish permission to reorder the conversation.

Record the answer in the existing brief/review notes, including fixed ranges,
whether internal cleanup or rearrangement is wanted, and any duration target.
These are editorial choices, not new runtime modes or schema fields. Choose
visual treatment, captions, aspect ratio and music separately: a cleaned-up
excerpt can have rich visuals, and a rebuilt story can use restrained visuals.
Preserve the requested length; an excerpt need not be forced under 60 seconds.

Good hooks and clarity matter in all three cases. Within permitted boundaries,
choose an engaging, understandable opening and a natural ending. Keep titles
and hook copy faithful to what the excerpt delivers. A podcast section may
deliver an interesting perspective, exchange, anecdote or explanation without
becoming a lesson or a newly constructed narrative. If an exact requested range
lacks context, identify the issue and offer a boundary adjustment; do not
silently add distant passages or manufacture a payoff to justify the excerpt.

The source-wide payoff-first method below applies when building a standalone
Short. Its sentence-level trimming guidance also applies when cleanup is
requested. Segment-only work preserves the interior unless the user expands
that scope. Review quality against the chosen edit, not a different deliverable.

## Select a complete message across the recording

### Plan independent production work in parallel

Aaron authorized production subagents on September 15 after the 56.24-second
Short took 65m11s. Delegate concrete independent work when it shortens the
critical path; retain one lead editor responsible for the coherent result.

Establish the story, source ranges and shared output clock before dependent
assembly. Then give one worker source/audio/caption preparation and another
reference/asset/visual preparation. Assign explicit file ownership, pass the
same brief and timing, and require workers to preserve each other's changes.
Reference scouting can begin while the lead evaluates source passages, but a
final visual sequence must follow the accepted cut. A fresh independent critic
still reviews the assembled candidate; its author cannot approve its own work.

Prepare review assets and documentation while supervised media work runs when
they do not mutate pinned inputs or need its unfinished output. Independent
encoded visual and audio reviews may overlap after encoding. Heavy rendering,
browser capture and media QC continue to obey the one-owner resource lease;
spawning agents is not permission to launch competing render trees.

Reuse catalog mechanisms and the existing project before bespoke construction.
Record the exact missing story relationship before building custom artwork.
Freeze the cut before spending on dependent graphics, and repair an affected
component instead of restarting selection and every completed stage.

Record the continuous start-to-handoff clock and each production lane's actual
start/end, including failures, waiting and integration. Parallel lane durations
overlap and must not be summed as elapsed delivery time. Export `delivery.json`
records its own preparation and execution only, not upstream editorial work.
Use the [staged export and resume path](NATIVE_SHORTS_WORKFLOW.md#resume-verification-without-repeating-media-generation)
for verification failures. Do not promise a future full-production time from a
prepared render or a synthetic test.

### Aaron's standing produced-clip preference — September 15, 2026

Aaron explicitly allows clips up to **90 seconds**. Use the shortest version
that gives a new viewer the subject, why it matters, the necessary development
and a complete payoff. Do not default to 20–30 seconds, discard useful reasoning
to get under a minute, or pad an already complete idea to reach 90 seconds.
The current brief can override this preference. Segment/excerpt and cleanup
requests retain their previously agreed scope.

For produced clips, visual storytelling is expected where it helps the important
explanation. Show the actual object, action, relationship or change through
relevant pictures, scrolling footage, source-board details or a HyperFrames
representation. When discussing downloading a skill, consider a visible skill
artifact, download, setup, attempted task and outcome, selecting only the beats
the speech supports. Use real sources when identified and available; an
illustrative workflow must not impersonate a real product or fabricate results.
Source permissions still apply. Keyword captions, text panels and zooms alone
do not satisfy this direction. Purposeful presenter holds remain useful.

Before selecting a produced assembly, answer from the retained source:

- Would a new viewer know what this is about and why it is worth watching?
- Does the title, actual opening speech and first visual open the same question?
- Which explanation earns the payoff, and did tightening remove any of it?
- What do the important visuals show that the captions alone do not?

Complete the Director's template/alternatives/conditions/critic workflow; a
canonical template fill does not establish a good hook. Give named entities
and showable actions speech-bound visual decisions instead of exempting the
whole clip as a personal account. Compare actual retained audio to captions
before expensive final renders. Known clipped syllables, substituted/missing
words or unresolved damaged joins require repair, not a handoff listening note.

### Assemble the source-supported story

The candidate is an assembled story, not necessarily one continuous time range.
A goal near the beginning, an obstacle later and a lesson or experiment near the
end can form one concise Short. Search the complete transcript for these parts
before declaring that a moment has no payoff. Remove intervening rambling and
repetition; preserve the reasoning that makes the ending meaningful.

Design from the payoff first. Viewing order follows what the audience needs:
context → problem → discovery is one option; discovery → context → explanation
is another. Recording order is not mandatory. Keep the actual claim, causal
relationships and speaker attribution intact when changing the order.

### What counts as a journey payoff

The Short can resolve its local question while the larger journey stays open.
An achieved commercial result or an instructional formula is not required.

| Payoff | What the assembled speech should make understandable |
|---|---|
| Learning or mistake | What the experience taught the creator and why it matters |
| Realization | What became clearer or changed in the creator's understanding |
| Decision | What the creator chose and the reason for that choice |
| Current experiment | What is being tried now, why, and what question it addresses |
| Progress | What changed, what it reveals, or how it affects the next move |

“Here is what I am trying and why” is a valid journey payoff. A specific tested
learning may be stronger, but do not discard an experiment because its result
is pending. A broad goal supplies context or stakes; connect it to the episode's
choice, attempt, realization or progress. Avoid imposing a how-to script's
actionable solution requirement on every journey story.

## Work the transcript before choosing cuts

1. **Map useful story parts across the whole source.** Note goals, current state,
   stakes, problems, evidence, attempts, reasons, realizations and next moves
   with their source ranges. Topic segments are navigation, not clip boundaries.
2. **Propose the payoff, then find its actual words.** State the viewer's local
   question and what will answer it. An editorial paraphrase explains the plan;
   it does not count as recorded dialogue. Search elsewhere in the recording
   for missing context or reasoning, including after the apparent endpoint.
3. **Build a paper edit in viewing order.** Join complete source phrases from
   different moments as needed. Read the resulting transcript as one piece.
   Cut redundant setup and side paths before cutting the useful explanation.
   Measure retained spans at source speed against the requested duration;
   under 60 seconds is a target when requested, not a reason to amputate a point.
4. **Compare assemblies, not only source moments.** Try a different opening,
   a later realization as the ending, or an earlier goal as context when that
   improves comprehension. A weak continuous passage may become a useful
   assembled story. Distinguish alternate versions from different takeaways.
5. **Check meaning across each jump.** Resolve “this,” “it,” “we” and “after
   that”; preserve their referents, dates, targets, speaker and certainty.
   Rearrangement must not make a planned action look completed, imply an
   unobserved result, or turn advice into someone else's experience. Cut timing
   and natural delivery still need actual audiovisual review.

For example, “I think we will hit it” about a near-term $1,000 target cannot
be moved directly beneath a $100,000 goal as if that were the same prediction.
A hypothetical example from the user guides the edit method; do not insert its
sample lesson unless the recording actually contains it.

### Test one point, then tighten the language inside it

Write the opening's local question and the ending's recorded answer side by
side. They must address the same point. An ending such as “what do I actually
disagree with?” does not fulfill an opening promising “my message in one
sentence.” A discovery of a useful question can be its own story, but that
story still needs context showing what changed; do not call every final
question a realization payoff.

Give each retained beat a job in that one story: context, obstacle, reason,
attempt, evidence or resolution. Related subject matter is not enough. An
education-to-proof story does not automatically need a pricing lesson in its
middle. Split competing points or choose the one that the recording supports
most clearly. A small observed change can complete an honest progress update
without becoming proof of success or evidence of what caused it.

Then do a sentence-level trimming pass across every candidate, including the
ones whose story already works:

- Remove empty “like,” “you know,” false starts, repeated intensifiers and
  redundant explanations when delivery allows it.
- Shorten unnecessary clauses and parallel examples before shortening the
  explanation that earns the point. Removing whole sections is only one form
  of editing; useful sentences can also become much tighter.
- Preserve uncertainty, negation, conditions, speaker identity and causal
  meaning. “I think,” “we plan” and “so that we can” may be essential claims,
  not filler. Keep the qualifications that make an opinion fair and accurate.
- Keep exact source subranges for every internal deletion. Read the resulting
  sentence continuously: it needs a subject, a complete claim and clear
  referents. Do not substitute an editor's paraphrase as recorded speech.
- Listen across internal joins before accepting the cut. ASR word timestamps
  are pointers, not safe audio boundaries; preserve natural cadence and enough
  breath. Use a longer phrase if a tiny splice sounds broken.

Do not treat a shorter duration as evidence of a complete thought. Check both
conciseness and whether the spoken ending fulfills the spoken opening. An
alternate speaker's real answer can support an assembled conversation, with
that speaker visible or clearly identified; it cannot become the creator's
own unspoken conclusion.

Keep a compact record in the existing shortlist or strategy:

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Viewer and local question | What makes this story understandable and relevant to a new viewer |
| Payoff and type | The learning, realization, decision, experiment or progress it delivers |
| Spoken assembly | Exact excerpts and ordered source ranges, including backward jumps |
| Development | How the retained reasons, evidence or events earn that payoff |
| Duration and joins | Sum of retained spans; uncertain timing or meaning at the seams |
| Visual opportunity | What can be shown at the important explanatory moments |
| Selection | Strength, missing material and overlaps with other assemblies |

If the source still cannot supply a coherent story after this assembly pass,
keep it as a concept for a new recording or reject it. Do not manufacture a
lesson or force a fixed number of clips. Conversely, do not reject useful
material because its setup and payoff are several minutes apart.

## Plan visual explanation at the important moments

For Aaron's produced journey Shorts, proactively look for visual explanations
where the speech becomes abstract, a board detail matters, or an example would
make the point easier to follow. His presence remains important; authenticity
does not mean uninterrupted full-frame talking or only keyword captions.
Respect an explicit trim-only or graphics-disabled request.

| Viewing need | Useful treatment |
|---|---|
| Follow an expression, admission or realization | Hold on the presenter; return to him for the personal turn |
| Hear the explanation while inspecting a detail | Presenter above with board/example below, or the inverse when it reads better |
| Understand a relationship or proposed change | Develop one diagram or offer through its states; retain context across cuts |
| See actual evidence or a procedure | Relevant footage, reference or screen demonstration with a readable result |
| Read something too dense for two panes | Brief full-frame detail, then return to the presenter |

A temporary presenter/board split is welcome when both panes have a useful job.
Choose relative sizes from face/gesture clarity and detail readability, not an
automatic 50/50 split. Crop to the relevant board area; briefly show the whole
board if orientation helps. If handwriting cannot read at phone size, use a
faithful explanatory redraw of verified content, not guessed text. A synchronized
second crop is a detail view, not an invented second camera.

For each selected visual, identify what the viewer should notice, what changes
or becomes clearer, the spoken cue, and when to return or change view. Examples:

- Keep one offer visible as $9 becomes the planned $19 at a 20-member threshold.
- Show the existing list and community, with outreach explicitly still to do,
  as the creator explains the next experiment.
- Show a relevant failed/result comparison when those actual examples exist;
  otherwise explain the work remaining with a diagram marked as explanatory.

Use supplied footage, including material outside the dialogue cut, and suitable
available references or examples. Follow current media-source permissions when
sourcing more. A real page can establish identity; a scrolling page or word
label alone does not necessarily explain the mechanism. Evaluate whether it
earns that time or needs a more focused visual treatment.

Cut and change composition when the idea, attention target or story beat changes.
There is no mandatory cut interval, B-roll percentage or all-video split layout.
Preserve useful holds and enough time to understand each new visual. Place
captions so the face, gesture and decisive detail stay readable.

## Review the assembled Short before polishing it

Apply Ralston's payoff criteria to this journey's actual promise: alignment,
actionable clarity (including usable understanding or a decision), insight depth
and cognitive resolution. Identify the words or visible action that earns each
judgment; a `payoff` label or plausible strategy summary is not evidence.

Check the opening's visual, written and spoken components together. Then watch
the complete assembly: can a cold viewer follow the jumps, understand what was
learned or is being tried, and feel a concise point land? Review whether each
important visual demonstrates, explains or usefully grounds that point. Treat
repetition, unclear references and empty delays as retention risks; actual
audience retention remains unmeasured until tested with viewers.

Keep selection review distinct from export integrity. Existing story validators
bind authored phases, media and timing; they do not certify semantic payoff or
visual effectiveness. Record incomplete listening or playback honestly. A local
editorial review can apply this playbook without a paid Director invocation.
